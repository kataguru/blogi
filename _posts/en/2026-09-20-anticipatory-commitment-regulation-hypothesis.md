## Hypothesis

Precommitments—rules of action defined in advance—improve long-term regulation in situations where decision-making capacity is known to weaken or where the consequences of choices are delayed, cumulative, or irreversible.

The mechanism is based on shifting the decision in time. The principle of action is chosen in a stable state of judgment before fatigue, urgency, emotional arousal, temptation, or another predictable state weakens judgment. Commitment does not require predicting an individual situation. It requires recognizing a recurring class of situations.

## Causal mechanism

Precommitment operates through the following chain:

1. a high-risk class of situations is identified in advance
2. a principle of action is chosen while judgment is reliable
3. the amount of deliberation and the number of choices required in the situation are reduced
4. harmful, inconsistent, and short-sighted decisions become less frequent
5. cumulative harm and the need for repeated corrective regulation decrease
6. the accumulation of allostatic load slows and recovery from adversity improves.

The final two steps form the physiological extension of the hypothesis. They cannot be inferred from behavioral change alone and must be demonstrated separately.

## Information basis

An appropriate rule is based on coarse-grained knowledge across four dimensions:

- **fragility profile:** which consequences the person is especially vulnerable to
- **weak states of judgment:** when their judgment becomes predictably unreliable
- **environmental base rates:** how often the class of situations occurs and what its typical distribution of outcomes is
- **temporal structure of consequences:** whether the effects are immediate, delayed, cumulative, or irreversible.

The quality of a rule is determined before its outcome is observed. Quality is defined by how well the rule targets a real risk, its unambiguity, proportionate binding force, feasibility, revision procedure, and recovery procedure after a breach.

## Scope conditions

Precommitment is most useful when at least one of the following conditions applies:

- decisions are made in a predictably weak state of judgment
- consequences accumulate slowly
- reliable feedback arrives only after a long delay
- an individual consequence may be difficult to reverse
- repeated decision-making creates a significant burden.

In situations with rapid, reliable feedback and in quickly changing environments, flexible and context-sensitive regulation may produce better results.

The rule is reviewed at defined intervals while judgment is stable. It is not changed while the person is in the situation from whose influence the rule is intended to protect them.

A single breach is handled through a recovery procedure chosen in advance. A lapse does not in itself invalidate the rule or justify abandoning it.

Maintaining rules requires time, energy, safety, and practical room to maneuver. Resource scarcity may both weaken adherence to commitments and increase allostatic load independently of them.

## Relationship to allostasis

Allostasis is predictive regulation in which the brain adjusts physiology and behavior to anticipated needs. Precommitment is a deliberate behavioral implementation of this principle: action is prepared before a predictable need for regulation arises, rather than only after an error or harm has occurred.

Precommitment is neither identical to allostasis nor an external counterpart to it. It is one possible way of implementing predictive regulation at the behavioral level.

## Testable predictions

**Assessment: the following predictions are derived from the hypothesis but have not been tested as a whole.**

- High-quality precommitments reduce harmful choices, particularly during predictably weak states of judgment.
- The effect is greatest in domains where consequences are delayed, cumulative, or irreversible.
- The effect is small or negative when feedback is rapid and the environment changes continuously.
- Rules with a revision cycle perform better in changing environments than permanently rigid rules.
- A recovery procedure reduces the probability that a single deviation leads to abandoning the commitment altogether.
- The effect of precommitments on allostatic load is mediated by reduced harmful exposure and a lower need for repeated corrective regulation.
- At least part of the effect remains after controlling for socioeconomic position, conscientiousness, health status, and available resources.

## Falsification criteria

The behavioral mechanism of the hypothesis is substantially weakened if preregistered intervention studies show that appropriate precommitments do not reduce harmful choices in the classes of situations for which they were designed.

The allostatic extension of the hypothesis is falsified if the behavioral effect occurs but repeated longitudinal or intervention studies show that it does not translate into slower accumulation of allostatic load or improved recovery.
