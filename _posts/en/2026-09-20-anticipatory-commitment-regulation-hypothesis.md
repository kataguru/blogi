## Hypothesis

Precommitments—rules of action defined in advance while judgment is stable—are one behavioral mechanism through which a person builds resilience.

They shift decisions away from situations in which judgment is known to weaken, stabilize action during a disturbance, limit cascading errors, and support a return to a functional state. At the same time, they may reduce harmful exposures and slow the accumulation of allostatic load.

Precommitments do not create resilience on their own. They operate together with flexible regulation and available resources:

- precommitment prevents predictable errors
- flexible regulation responds to new and changing situations
- resources make it possible to absorb a disturbance and recover from it.

Resilience emerges from the interaction of these three elements.

## Definition of resilience

In this hypothesis, resilience means the ability of a system to:

1. maintain adequate functioning during a disturbance
2. prevent a local disturbance from spreading into wider damage
3. return to a functional state after the disturbance
4. change its operating model when the environment or its fragility profile changes.

Precommitment particularly supports the first three functions. Revision and flexible regulation are essential for the fourth.

## Conceptual scope

The hypothesis primarily concerns principles of action that a person sets for themselves. A rule may derive its binding force from the person's own decision or from an environment they have arranged themselves.

The hypothesis does not, by itself, cover commitment mechanisms based on external punishment, financial loss, reputation, or social monitoring. These may produce similar behavior, but their mechanisms are partly different.

Breaking a rule and making a harmful choice are not the same variable. A harmful choice is a decision that, over a predefined time horizon, increases the risk or amount of a consequence that the person has reflectively decided to avoid.

## Normative premise

The hypothesis concerns situations in which a person has deliberately endorsed a principle formed while judgment is stable as the reference point for their later conduct.

A stable state is not automatically correct, nor is a preference formed in that state objectively superior. Its status rests on the person's own prior decision.

The rule includes revision and exception conditions through which new information, a changed environment, and genuinely changed preferences can be taken into account. However, the rule is not changed in the very situation from whose predictable influence it is intended to protect the person, unless a predefined exception condition is met.

## Causal mechanisms

Precommitment is expected to support resilience through three complementary pathways.

### 1. Stabilizing action

1. a high-risk class of situations is identified in advance
2. a principle of action is defined while judgment is reliable
3. the rule reduces the deliberation required in a weak state or restricts the available options
4. the probability of harmful choices decreases
5. functioning is better preserved during the disturbance.

### 2. Containing the disturbance and recovering

1. a predefined recovery procedure is attached to the rule
2. an individual breach is recognized without invalidating the entire rule
3. the deviation does not expand into a cascade
4. behavior returns more quickly to a stable course
5. the total cost of the disturbance and the resources required for recovery decrease.

### 3. Limiting allostatic load

1. harmful choices decrease
2. the amount or dose of harmful exposure decreases
3. the frequency, intensity, or duration of physiological activation associated with the exposure decreases
4. the accumulation of multisystem dysregulation, or allostatic load, slows
5. more resources remain available for recovery and future disturbances.

Each stage in the chain is a separate variable. A behavioral effect alone does not demonstrate a physiological effect, and a short-term physiological change alone does not demonstrate a reduction in allostatic load.

## Information basis

An appropriate rule is based on coarse-grained knowledge across four dimensions:

- **fragility profile:** the consequences to which the person or system is particularly vulnerable
- **weak states of judgment:** when and how strongly judgment becomes unreliable
- **environmental base rates:** how often the class of situations occurs and what its typical distribution of outcomes is
- **temporal structure of consequences:** whether the effects are immediate, delayed, cumulative, or difficult to reverse.

Commitment does not require precise prediction of an individual situation. It is sufficient to identify a recurring class of situations, its typical course, and one's own vulnerability to it with adequate reliability.

## Rule quality

The quality of a rule is assessed before its outcome is observed. Quality criteria include:

- targeting a real and sufficiently stable risk
- an unambiguous scope and trigger condition
- proportionality to the intended benefit
- practical feasibility
- predefined exception conditions
- revision at defined intervals
- a recovery procedure after a breach.

A rule is not defined as high quality merely because it produced the desired outcome.

## Failure mechanism

Precommitment does not inherently increase resilience.

A misdirected, outdated, or excessively rigid rule may replace an occasional error of judgment with a systematic error. It may consume resources, prevent the use of new information, and weaken adaptation to changed conditions.

The direction of the effect is therefore expected to depend on rule quality:

- a well-targeted rule supports resilience
- an irrelevant rule has no effect or wastes resources
- a poorly targeted or unrevised rule may weaken resilience.

Precommitment without flexible revision creates brittle stability: the system withstands an expected disturbance but may fail when confronted with a novel one.

## Scope conditions

The benefit of precommitment depends on at least the following factors:

- the magnitude of variation in judgment
- the frequency of weak states of judgment
- the delay and reliability of feedback
- the accumulation and reversibility of consequences
- environmental stability
- rule quality
- available resources.

Large variation in judgment may increase both the benefit of a rule in a single situation and the number of situations in which the rule is needed. These effects must be distinguished from each other.

Delayed feedback and difficult-to-reverse consequences increase the potential value of precommitment only when the class of situations remains sufficiently stable and the rule continues to target the relevant risk.

In situations with rapid, reliable feedback and in rapidly changing environments, flexible and context-sensitive judgment may produce better results.

## Breach and revision

A single rule breach is handled through a recovery procedure chosen in advance. A breach does not by itself invalidate the rule or justify abandoning it.

The recovery procedure is a central resilience mechanism. Its purpose is to prevent a local deviation from becoming a broader loss of functioning.

The rule is reviewed at defined intervals while judgment is stable. The review assesses at least:

- changes in the risk
- continued fit between the rule and its target
- harms caused by the rule
- feasibility
- the operation of exception conditions
- the effectiveness of the recovery procedure.

## Resource condition

Forming, remembering, following, and reviewing rules requires time, energy, safety, and practical room to maneuver.

Resource scarcity may:

- weaken rule quality
- reduce adherence
- increase the frequency of weak states of judgment
- slow recovery
- increase physiological strain independently of the rules.

Precommitment may protect resources, but maintaining it also consumes them. A rule supports resilience only when the resources it saves and the harm it prevents exceed its maintenance cost.

## Relationship to allostasis

Allostasis is predictive regulation through which the brain adjusts physiology and behavior to anticipated needs.

Precommitment is one deliberate behavioral implementation of this principle: action is prepared before a predictable need for regulation arises rather than only after an error or harm has occurred.

Precommitment is neither identical to allostasis nor an external counterpart to it. It is one way of implementing predictive regulation at the behavioral level.

Reducing allostatic load is one pathway through which precommitment may support resilience. It is neither the only pathway nor a synonym for resilience.

## Testable predictions

**Assessment: the following predictions are derived from the hypothesis but have not been tested as a whole.**

1. Precommitment reduces the probability of predefined harmful choices, particularly during weak states of judgment.
2. Precommitment improves the preservation of functioning during the kind of disturbance for which the rule was designed.
3. A recovery procedure reduces the probability that a single breach leads to further breaches or abandonment of the commitment.
4. A rule with a recovery procedure shortens recovery time compared with a rule without one.
5. A reduction in harmful choices mediates a reduction in the amount or dose of harmful exposure.
6. A reduction in harmful exposure mediates a reduction in repeated physiological activation.
7. A sufficiently sustained reduction in exposure and physiological activation predicts slower accumulation of allostatic load.
8. Lower allostatic load and reduced resource consumption predict better preservation of functioning and faster recovery from later disturbances.
9. The direction of the effect depends on rule quality assessed in advance: a well-targeted rule helps, whereas a poorly targeted rule may cause harm.
10. Rules with a revision cycle support resilience in a changing environment better than permanently rigid rules.
11. Benefit per unit of time increases with the frequency of weak states of judgment, provided that the rule remains appropriate and feasible.
12. The benefit of rules weakens or becomes negative when their maintenance cost exceeds the resources they conserve and the harm they prevent.
13. The observed effects remain at least directionally consistent when major alternative explanations, including health status, socioeconomic position, conscientiousness, and available resources, are taken into account.

## Falsification criteria

The behavioral core of the hypothesis is substantially weakened if rules assessed in advance as appropriate and high quality repeatedly fail to reduce predefined harmful choices in the weak states of judgment for which they were designed.

The resilience claim is substantially weakened if rules change choices but repeatedly fail to:

- preserve functioning during a disturbance
- contain cascading disturbances
- accelerate the return to a functional state
- conserve resources for later disturbances.

The mechanism attributed to the recovery procedure is falsified if it does not reduce cascading breaches or shorten recovery time.

The physiological extension is falsified if harmful exposures decrease over a sustained period but the associated repeated physiological activation or accumulation of allostatic load does not decrease when measured with appropriate indicators and time horizons.

The overall model is falsified if precommitments fail to explain the preservation of functioning, containment of disturbances, or recovery regardless of rule quality, variation in judgment, environmental stability, and resources.
